Phishing is the most common vector for account compromise across every industry, and Mortega is not immune. Most successful attacks exploit two things: urgency and impersonation. Once you know the patterns, they become much easier to spot before any damage is done. The guidance below will help you identify suspicious messages and respond correctly whether you caught the threat in time or already clicked.

## Common patterns

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Urgency and pressure">
    Messages that threaten immediate consequences — "Your account will be suspended in 24 hours," "Action required before midnight" — are designed to make you act before you think. Legitimate internal systems and services do not operate this way. When you feel pressured to act immediately, slow down and verify through a separate channel before doing anything.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Mismatched sender domains">
    An email claiming to come from a trusted company but sent from a domain like `secure-bank-alert.net` is a clear red flag. Always check the **full email address**, not just the display name — attackers frequently set a convincing display name while the actual sending domain reveals the fraud.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Requests for credentials">
    No legitimate internal Mortega system will ever ask you to enter your password via a link in an email. If clicking a link takes you to a login page you were not already expecting to visit, close the tab immediately and report the message.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Unexpected attachments">
    Do not open attachments you were not explicitly expecting, particularly files with extensions like `.exe`, `.zip`, or macro-enabled Office formats (`.docm`, `.xlsm`). These are common delivery mechanisms for malware. If a colleague appears to have sent you an unexpected file, confirm with them directly before opening it.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## What to do

### If you receive a suspicious message

<Steps>
  <Step title="Do not interact with the message">
    Do not click any links, download attachments, or reply to the sender. Even clicking "unsubscribe" in a phishing email can confirm that your address is active.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Report it">
    Use the **Report Phishing** button in your email client, or forward the message to `security@mortega.com`. Reporting helps protect your colleagues if the same campaign is targeting others at Mortega.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Delete the message">
    Once you have reported it, delete the message from your inbox and trash.
  </Step>
</Steps>

### If you clicked a link or entered your credentials

<Steps>
  <Step title="Change your password immediately">
    Go directly to the affected system and change your password without delay. Do not use the link you clicked to do this — navigate there yourself.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Notify the security team">
    Email `security@mortega.com` right away. Include the URL you visited and a brief description of what happened. The security team needs this information to assess the scope of the incident.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Do not wait to see what happens">
    Speed is critical. Even if nothing seems wrong immediately, attackers can establish persistence within minutes of obtaining credentials. Report first and investigate later.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
  If you entered your credentials on a suspicious page, treat your account as compromised until the security team confirms otherwise. Change your password before doing anything else.
</Warning>

## Simulated phishing tests

IT runs periodic simulated phishing campaigns to keep awareness sharp across the organization. If you click a simulated phishing link, you will be directed to a short training module. This is not punitive — the goal is awareness, not punishment.

<Note>
  If you notice an unusual volume of phishing messages targeting your account specifically — not just general spam — notify the security team at `security@mortega.com`. Targeted attacks against a specific employee warrant a different level of response.
</Note>
